The two-question filter: what deserves a save

Founder notes · Manu Parasuraman

Before blaming your backlog, audit your intake. Most people save with zero friction, which means the save button records appetite, not intent. Two questions, asked in the two seconds before saving, change the composition of the entire library.

Question one: does this imply an action?

A shelf-repair video implies buying hinges and cutting boards. A workout Reel implies a session tomorrow morning. If the answer is yes, the save should arrive as a project — steps, estimates, a first task. If the answer is no, that is fine, but the save should not pretend to be a project. This is why ORANO classifies every save as action, understand, or reference: a documentary gets key points worth keeping, not a fake checklist.

Question two: will this matter in thirty days?

Not "is this interesting?" — interesting is free and infinite. Will the future you need this? A lecture series you are genuinely working through passes. The fifteenth morning-routine video fails. What fails the filter should not be saved at all, and what stops passing later should be deleted without ceremony in the weekly review.

What the filter does to the library

Run both questions and the library shrinks and sharpens at the same time. Fewer saves, higher hit rate. Search gets easier, the knowledge graph gets cleaner, and — if you let your AI agent read your library through ORANO's read-only MCP server — the context you hand your tools gets dramatically more useful. Garbage in, garbage generated.
