Repair videos into repair checklists: doing the DIY instead of rewatching it

Founder notes · Manu Parasuraman

There is a genre of video every household knows: the one that shows exactly how to fix the thing. It gets watched twice — once to feel prepared, once on the floor of the utility room with the machine in pieces — and it is still not a plan. The information was all there. The plan was not.

What a repair video actually contains

Watched as evidence rather than entertainment, a repair video yields four lists: the parts (with sizes and part numbers shouted out or shown on screen), the tools, the steps in order, and the traps ("don't overtighten", "photograph the wiring first"). Extracting those by pausing and re-scrubbing costs twenty minutes of a task you already didn't want to do. So nobody does it — and the video gets rewatched on the floor, phone in one hand, wrench in the other.

What the checklist changes

When the save becomes a structured project, the four lists arrive extracted and ordered: an estimated total time before you start, a parts-and-cost line you can order from, steps sized to realistic work sessions, and the traps attached to the steps where they bite. The phone stays in your pocket between steps. The repair becomes a thing you execute instead of a thing you imitate.

This is exactly the flow ORANO automates: share the video, get the understanding — speech, on-screen text, and caption read separately — and receive an ordered task list with time and cost estimates when the content is actionable. Same for furniture assembly, car maintenance, appliance installs. The app is free on iOS and Android.

Fix the thing. Keep the video as credit, not as crutch.
