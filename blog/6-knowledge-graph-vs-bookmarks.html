A knowledge graph vs a bookmarks folder

Founder notes · Manu Parasuraman

A bookmark answers one question: "where was that thing?" A knowledge graph answers a different question entirely: "what do the things I saved say about each other, and about me?"

What a folder can't do

  • Tell you that the pgvector tutorial you saved connects to the retrieval-augmentation talk from last month.
  • Remember that you prefer Python examples, work in short sessions, and have been learning Italian for a year.
  • Surface the one saved repo that solves the problem you are staring at today.

Folders are flat. Understanding is not.

What a graph looks like in practice

In ORANO, every save is understood on arrival: summary, grounded key points, category, difficulty, time and cost estimates. On top of that sit layers that compound: curated memory facts (your preferences, skills, goals — with confidence and freshness scoring), a knowledge graph of concepts, tools, people and frameworks pulled from your saves, and semantic embeddings so search means "find me things like this" instead of "find me this exact title."

The practical test: ask a question you have never asked before. "What have I saved about pricing page design?" In a folder you scroll. In a graph, you get an answer assembled from three videos, two articles and one repo — with the reasoning attached.

The compounding effect

Every structured save makes the next answer better. That is the difference between an archive that grows and a memory that compounds.
