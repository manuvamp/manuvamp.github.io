Timestamps are citations

Founder notes · Manu Parasuraman

A note taken from a video without a timestamp is a rumor. It says "the speaker claimed X" and dares you to re-watch forty minutes to verify it. With a minute-mark attached, the same note becomes a citation: checkable in fifteen seconds, linkable in a doc, defensible in a meeting.

Why summaries lose their timestamps

Human note-takers drop timestamps because they're writing prose, and prose flows without coordinates. AI summaries drop them because nobody asks. The fix is to make the timestamp a requirement of the note format — every claim carries its source minute, the way an academic paragraph carries its page number.

What changes when timestamps exist

Three things. Verification gets cheap, so disagreements about "what the video said" end in seconds. Sharing gets surgical — you send the link at 23:40 instead of recommending the whole hour. And trust compounds: a library where every claim can be checked is one your team actually relies on. ORANO attaches timestamps to every key point and flashcard it generates for this exact reason.

The payoff

Your video library stops being a collection of plays and becomes a set of checkable sources — the difference between having watched something and being able to use it.
